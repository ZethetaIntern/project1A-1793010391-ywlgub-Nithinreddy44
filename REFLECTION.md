# Engineering Reflection: Designing PayScale HTTPE

## 1. Navigating Architectural Trade-offs Under Extreme Scale

Designing the High-Throughput Transaction Processing Engine (HTTPE) for PayScale Financial Technologies has been an intensive exercise in balancing theoretical elegance against hard operational realities. The central engineering challenge—scaling transaction throughput by a factor of 10x (from 1,200 to 12,000+ sustained TPS and 18,000 burst TPS) ahead of a critical 15-day Diwali festive launch—demanded uncompromising prioritization of financial correctness, resilience, and budget discipline.

The most critical architectural decision centered on concurrency control and database selection. The legacy system relied on pessimistic row locks (`SELECT ... FOR UPDATE`), which collapsed under concurrent P2P transfers, producing a 15% deadlock rate. In a distributed multi-shard banking engine, distributed locking across physical nodes via Two-Phase Commit (2PC) introduces prohibitive commit latency (45–90 ms) and creates severe availability vulnerabilities under network partitions. By transitioning to **Optimistic Concurrency Control (OCC) with atomic Compare-And-Swap (CAS) version vectors**, we unlocked linear horizontal scaling across 4 database shards, achieving sub-15ms local commit times while mathematically eliminating row deadlocks. Proving that OCC and double-entry bookkeeping strictly prevent double-spend and write-skew anomalies reinforced that correctness must be structurally embedded in the data model rather than retrofitted through application patches.

Similarly, evaluating messaging technologies highlighted the danger of treating benchmarks in isolation. While Amazon SQS FIFO appeared operationally straightforward, its 3,000 TPS batching ceiling and per-request pricing ($11,000+/month for 85M daily transactions) would have consumed a quarter of our total $45,000 budget. Conversely, Apache Kafka with KRaft consensus and 32 partitions easily absorbs 60,000+ msg/sec write spikes while remaining well within our compute allocation ($3,600/month). Coupling Kafka with the Transactional Outbox pattern and Debezium CDC solved the distributed dual-write dilemma, ensuring that financial events are never dropped or duplicated, even during unexpected node crashes.

## 2. Key Learnings & Institutional Resilience

Another profound realization was the necessity of designing for failure as a steady-state condition rather than an edge case. The FMEA analysis covering 25 failure modes revealed that complex distributed systems rarely fail catastrophically in a single component; rather, unhandled latencies in minor peripheral services (such as notification delivery or fraud scoring) cascade upstream, saturating connection pools and thread workers. Implementing sliding-window circuit breakers (`CB-FRAUD`, `CB-NOTIFY`), bulkhead thread pool isolation, and PgBouncer connection multiplexing transformed potential systemic collapses into contained, graceful degradations with automated fast-fails.

Finally, managing the financial constraints of an infrastructure ceiling ($45,000/month) required precise capacity modeling. Sizing compute, memory, and IOPS across three operating scales proved that high throughput does not necessitate uncontrolled cloud sprawl. By pairing 4 right-sized PostgreSQL shards on NVMe storage with Redis clustering, we achieved 12,000 TPS for $40,850/month—leaving a 9.2% operating buffer.

Ultimately, this project demonstrated that resilient fintech architecture is not about seeking impossible distributed consensus across all components, but about orchestrating autonomous, locally consistent shards through deterministic sagas, immutable audit trails, and automated compensating reversals.
